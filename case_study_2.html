🗂️ Case Study 2: The doc & query agent

The pitch: stop being the human search engine for your own team's documentation.

What was actually going on

Here's the pattern I kept noticing. Someone on the team would ask "wait, did we already decide this?" or "where's this documented?" and the answer was usually "let me check" followed by ten minutes of digging, or worse, someone answering from memory and getting it slightly wrong. Multiply that by a team, by months, and it adds up to a real amount of lost time. At NBBL this shows up constantly with partner banks and aggregators asking questions that are already sitting in an integration spec somewhere.

The second half of the problem was documentation itself. Every new feature needed the same four things: a proposal note, a BRD, a Jira story, and release notes. And every time, I was starting from a blank page, which meant the quality and structure varied depending on how much time I had that week.

Who this is actually for

Mostly it's for PMs and engineers on my own team who want a fast, trustworthy answer without having to track down a specific person. There's a secondary audience too: partner-facing folks who field the same repeated questions from banks and aggregators and would rather point to something reliable than reconstruct an answer each time.

What I built

Two things under one roof, really.

A query agent that only answers from what it's actually been given. Someone emails a question, the agent searches the project's document set, and if it finds an answer it cites exactly where (file, section, slide, whatever). If it can't find one, it says so, plainly, and only goes looking on the open web if I say yes. It runs on an hourly schedule so it's checking for new questions in the background without me having to remember to open it.

A documentation skill that turns one sentence into a full package. I tell it "build the docs for X" and it comes back with a proposal note, a BRD, a Jira story, and release notes, all tied together with a shared requirement ID (things like FR‑1, NFR‑2, AC‑3) so the acceptance criteria in Jira actually trace back to the requirement in the BRD instead of drifting apart the way they usually do.

💡 The part I'm proudest of isn't the generation, it's the refusal. Most doc tools either ask you a hundred questions or quietly make things up. This one asks once, states any assumption it has to make right at the top of the document, and moves on. That balance took a few tries to get right.

The tradeoffs I actually made

I thought about this longer than I expected to. Here's the table, and then the one decision I'll actually defend if someone pushes back on it.

DecisionWhat I didn't do insteadWhy
Hard rule: only answer from the documents providedLet it fall back on general knowledge when stuckA confident wrong answer does more damage than an honest "I don't know." I'd rather it decline.
Ask once, then commit to stated assumptionsKeep asking follow ups until every detail is nailed downNobody sticks with a tool that interrogates them. One clarifying round, then it moves.
Email as the interfaceA dedicated chat UIZero onboarding. People already live in their inbox, so I met them there.
Draft first, send only after I approveFully autonomous replies from day oneTrust gets earned, not assumed. I wanted to watch it work before letting it work alone.

I also considered just using ChatGPT or a generic assistant for this — here's why I didn't: Neither ChatGPT nor Cursor gives you a project scoped knowledge base with per document citations plus scheduled, email triggered execution in one place. Existing RPA style tools are great at rigid workflows and terrible at anything that needs judgment. Claude Cowork was the first place I found all three pieces (knowledge base, reusable skill, scheduled unattended execution) living together.

The tradeoff I knowingly accepted: email is a weaker interaction model than a real chat interface. No live back and forth, no rich formatting. I took that hit on purpose because the alternative (a new tool people have to remember to open) usually just doesn't get used.

Where I drew the line for v1

In scope: grounded Q&A with citations, scheduled email checking, the four document skill with shared requirement IDs, and human review before anything gets sent out.

Left out on purpose: multiple languages, automatic pulling from live systems (I add documents by hand for now), a Slack or Teams version, and anything beyond a single approver.

None of those exclusions were because they're not valuable. They just weren't necessary to prove the core idea works, and shipping something smaller and real beat shipping something bigger and theoretical.

How it actually behaves under the hood

It takes either an email question or a one line feature request as input. It only knows what's in the project's document set. If the documents support an answer, it cites the source and replies in thread. If they don't, it says so and offers to search the web, but only acts on that after I confirm. For documentation requests, unresolved details become explicit written assumptions rather than something it goes back to ask about again. Every so often two documents will disagree with each other, and rather than quietly picking one, it flags the conflict and cites both, which has actually been useful for catching stale docs.

Where I'd take this next: eventually I want it pulling straight from a live document repository instead of me updating it by hand, a Slack native version so people don't need to switch to email, and a multi approver flow for anything that touches compliance, which matters a lot in a regulated payments environment like mine.

What surprised me most: almost all my design time went into teaching the agent to say no — refuse to guess, refuse to quietly pick a side, refuse to keep asking forever. None of that's glamorous, but it's why I actually use this daily instead of admiring it once and moving on.

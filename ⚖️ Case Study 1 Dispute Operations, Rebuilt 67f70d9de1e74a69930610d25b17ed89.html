⚖️ Case Study 1: Dispute Operations, Rebuilt

The pitch: a consumer dispute should move like a product workflow, not disappear into handoffs between teams.

Details have been anonymized to protect partner and operational confidentiality.

What was actually going on

A consumer sees one failed payment. Behind it, there can be a chain of systems, participants, status changes, and decisions.

The complaint may begin in one place, the transaction evidence may sit somewhere else, and the refund or reversal may depend on a third party. Meanwhile, operations teams are trying to answer the only question that matters to the consumer: “What is happening to my money?”

The old workflow was not broken because people did not care. It was broken because the operating model relied too heavily on people remembering what came next.

A case could move between intake, transaction validation, reconciliation, partner follow-up, adjudication, and refund without a shared view of:

  • the current state of the case;
  • the person or team accountable for the next action;
  • the evidence needed to move forward;
  • the service-level clock;
  • the correct escalation path when the normal path failed.

That made the work slower than it needed to be, and it made exceptions disproportionately expensive. The difficult cases consumed the most time precisely because they were the least structured.

Who this is actually for

Consumers need a resolution process that feels predictable, even when the payment ecosystem behind it is not.

Operations teams need to see the full case context without manually stitching together records across systems and participants.

Partner banks and payment aggregators need clear ownership, evidence requirements, and escalation rules.

Risk and compliance teams need traceability: who made a decision, on what basis, and whether the process followed the required controls.

Product and engineering teams need to know where the workflow is failing—not just how many complaints arrived.

What I changed

I treated a dispute as a stateful product workflow rather than a collection of queues.

The core change was a shared case model. Every dispute was organized around a common case record with the transaction reference, dispute reason, current lifecycle state, assigned owner, evidence status, SLA timer, reconciliation status, and final outcome.

That meant a case could be understood as one journey:

Intake → validation → evidence collection → reconciliation → decision → refund / closure → exception escalation

Instead of relying on informal coordination, the workflow made the next required action explicit.

I also redesigned the ownership model around failure types. A case should not wait because three teams assume another team is investigating it. For each major exception class, the workflow identified:

  • the accountable owner;
  • the evidence required to progress;
  • the expected turnaround time;
  • the escalation trigger;
  • the final decision or closure state.

The aim was not to automate judgment away. The aim was to remove unnecessary ambiguity so that people could spend their time on the cases that genuinely needed judgment.

The most important product decision was giving every case one visible state, one next action, and one accountable owner.

How the workflow behaves

  1. A dispute is raised and linked to the relevant transaction reference.
  1. The system validates the information needed to begin investigation.
  1. The case enters a defined lifecycle state, with a current owner and SLA.
  1. Reconciliation and evidence checks determine whether the case can proceed, needs partner input, or qualifies for a specific exception path.
  1. If the case is delayed or blocked, escalation is triggered based on the failure class and ageing threshold—not on someone noticing an email.
  1. The case is adjudicated, refunded or closed, with the decision trail retained for operations, risk, and compliance review.

The key object was the case itself:

Consumer complaint → transaction reference → case state → owner → evidence → reconciliation result → decision → outcome

Once that model was clear, the screens, reports, and handoffs had something consistent to reflect.

What could go wrong, and what I did about it

A structured workflow makes genuine exceptions harder to handle.

The answer was not to create a rigid “happy-path only” process. It was to define explicit exception states and escalation paths, so unusual cases were visible rather than handled outside the system.

Partners interpret the same failure differently.

The workflow needed common definitions, required evidence, and named owners. A shared status without shared meaning would only create false clarity.

Faster resolution comes at the cost of poor decisions.

Speed was not the only metric. The design preserved the points where human judgment, risk review, and compliance controls were needed.

Operations teams work around the new flow.

A workflow only works when it reflects real operations. I focused on reducing handoffs and repeated checks, rather than adding process for its own sake.

The team measures closure, not customer confidence.

A closed case is not necessarily a resolved experience. Resolution time, status clarity, and a defensible outcome all mattered.

Where I would take it next

The next version would focus on proactive operations rather than only faster case handling:

  • Predict likely dispute bottlenecks from transaction and case patterns
  • Surface repeat failure modes by partner, reason code, or transaction stage
  • Introduce self-service status visibility for consumers and partner teams
  • Add a structured feedback loop from dispute outcomes into reliability and product-roadmap prioritisation
  • Use the operating data to identify which failure modes deserve platform investment rather than better manual handling

What this taught me

The real fix here was accountability, not a UI.

When a payment fails, the consumer does not care which system, partner, or queue owns the next step. They care whether the company can explain what happened and resolve it quickly.

The work taught me to model the operating process before designing the interface. Once the state model, ownership rules, and escalation logic were clear, the product became easier to build and the operation became easier to run.

That is the kind of work I enjoy most: taking a high-volume, high-stakes process that people have learned to tolerate, and turning it into a system that is measurable, explainable, and built to scale.
